What the Evidence Says

The load-bearing findings from conversion research. "Conversion" means a visitor completing the page's one goal — here, booking the confidential conversation. A "CTA" (call to action) is the button or link that asks them to do it. Full digests with all sources: conversion_science_digest.md best_sites_structures_digest.md hooks_and_entrance_effects_digest.md
1There is no magic section count — there is a required question sequenceStrong
No credible study says "5 sections convert best." What 10,000+ experiments (MECLABS, the research arm behind MarketingSherpa) DO show: the page must answer the visitor's internal questions in order — Where am I? What is this? Why should I care? Why you and no one else? What does it cost me to act? A page rebuilt around this sequencing produced +638% conversions in a documented teardown. Section count follows from how many questions your offer must answer — that is why the three propositions below have 7, 5, and 9 sections, each derived, not guessed.
2Expensive, rational decisions justify longer pages — but attention is front-loadedStrong
For cheap impulse offers, short pages win. For expensive, trust-heavy decisions, longer copy outperforms (CXL; a documented case: a page ~20× longer converted +30%). But eyetracking (Nielsen Norman Group) shows 57% of viewing time happens above the fold — the part visible before scrolling — and 74% within the first two screens. Verdict: length is allowed, burying key arguments is punished. The hook, who-this-is-for, and first CTA must live in screens 1–2.
3One action, one verb, repeated — never variedStrong
Pages with 1 link convert at 13.5% vs 10.5% for pages with 5+ links (Unbounce, 44,000+ pages). Elite sites obey this instinctively: Private Medical's entire site has one CTA word — "Inquire" — in three places. Repeating the same action 3–4 times down the page counts as one action. No menu of options, no secondary offers, no link farm.
4Clarity beats cleverness — concrete beats vagueStrong
Replacing clever messaging with plain clarity produced a +200% lift (MECLABS). A meta-analysis of 8,977 headline experiments (Nature, 2024): concrete headlines beat vague ones; pure curiosity-gap headlines cost trust. The hook may intrigue — but the visitor must decode what this is and for whom in one scan. Intrigue rides on top of clarity, never instead of it. This is why "you've optimized everything except what matters most" felt too vague — the fix is a concrete noun in the second half.
5Trust forms in 50 milliseconds — restraint IS the premium signalStrong
Visual-appeal judgments form within 50ms and halo onto credibility (Lindgaard 2006). Google's research: low visual complexity + looking recognizably like "a serious professional's page" drives that instant trust. Calm, dark, spare, typographic — the champagne-on-black direction is exactly what this evidence rewards.
6Proof works hardest for expensive services — and belongs NEXT to the askStrong
Displaying reviews raises purchase probability up to 270% — and the effect is larger for expensive offers (+380%) than cheap ones (Spiegel Research Center, Northwestern). Placement tests: a specific testimonial adjacent to the CTA lifts click-through 15–40% — proof at the moment of hesitation beats a testimonial dump at the bottom. Also: trust peaks BELOW perfection — a wall of flawless praise reads as fake; 82% of buyers actively look for the negative signal.
7Anonymous praise reads fabricated — specificity is the trust carrierAdapted
Testimonials without name, role, and context read as invented. For a confidential practice the evidence-compatible substitute is verifiable specificity: "CEO, industrial manufacturing, 240 employees" — plus an explicit statement of WHY names are withheld. Confidentiality itself, stated as policy, becomes proof of discretion (the Clare Locke move: "our biggest victories are the stories that never get published").
8For this audience, anxiety is the dominant brake — not motivationStrong
In the MECLABS conversion formula, anxiety subtracts with double force. A CEO considering this page fears exposure, being sold to, and wasted time — more than he doubts the value. Every ask must carry its anxiety-dissolvers in the same breath: confidential, 20–30 minutes, here is exactly what happens next. This is also why the entry is a "confidential conversation", never an "assessment".
9Real quantity scarcity works on CEOs — urgency theatrics backfireStrong
A meta-analysis of 131 studies: scarcity reliably raises purchase intention, and limited-quantity beats limited-time for uniqueness-seeking buyers — the CEO profile exactly. But Nielsen Norman Group documents that manufactured urgency (countdowns, "2 spots left!" popups) destroys trust with sophisticated users. The winning form: "The practice holds twelve private engagements" — stated once, calmly, as fact. Which it is.
10Blocking intro animations measurably cost conversions — the effect must be an overlayStrong
Google/Deloitte across 37 brands: a 0.1-second improvement in mobile load speed lifted lead-form submissions +8.3%. Nielsen Norman Group: splash intros are the most-rejected pattern on the web — users hunt for "skip". Your entrance-effect directive survives this evidence with one engineering law: the page paints fully underneath; the effect plays on a removable layer on top; any tap skips it; it runs once. Spectacle without the toll-gate. Full spec in Entrance Effect.

Deliverables menus ("12 sessions, email support, worksheets" — mid-market packaging). Stock photography. Scroll-hijacking and per-section animation spectacle. Explaining what coaching is — elite pages assume a sophisticated reader and speak to his situation immediately.
Propositions

Three Structures

All three obey the same evidence spine: hook → recognition → mechanism → proof → terms → ask, with the identical CTA repeated and proof beside every ask. They differ in depth and risk posture — how much each visitor question gets its own room. Section counts are derived in each proposition, per Evidence 1.
★The Recognition Section — your priority section, present in all three
The section you asked for — what this is, who this is for, in what situations — is the conversion engine of the page, and in every structure it sits at position 2, inside the first two screens where 74% of attention lives (E2). Its form: a quiet headline ("We work with CEOs.") followed by named situations the reader can find himself in — carrying a divorce or a private crisis while running the company · decision load that never stops · loneliness at the top · success that stopped feeling like anything · the moment everything breaks at once. Each situation one line, forensic, dignity-safe (status-safety rule). Verified statistics from the evidence arsenal make the reader feel statistically normal, which dissolves shame — half of CEOs report loneliness; 61% say it hinders performance; two-thirds receive no outside advice. It closes with one honest exclusion line (who this is not for), which builds trust and protects the calendar (E6, P6).

The Opening Line

The Hook — 8 Candidates

Built from formulas extracted from 22 real lines in the executive space (full catalog: hooks digest). The consensus on skeptical executives: the hook's job is recognition, not education — one sentence so precise it feels like being seen. It must be status-safe: the reader agrees without admitting weakness. Quiet confidence is the pattern interrupt. These are directional candidates for your ruling — final copy passes through the full voice-guide merge afterward.
A"You've optimized everything except the mind that runs it all."
Formula: The Asymmetry. Your own line, sharpened — the vague "what matters most" replaced with a concrete noun (E4: concrete beats vague). Names his blind spot in his own language (optimization) and points directly at the product: the mind itself.
B"Who do you talk to?"
Formula: The Implicating Question. Four words. The question money and title cannot answer — it bypasses the "I can handle this" reflex and lands directly on the featured problem, loneliness at the top. Highest risk/reward: devastating for the reader who feels it, opaque for the one who doesn't. Strongest paired with the Recognition section immediately below.
C"Surrounded all day. Alone with what matters."
Formula: Crowded-Calendar Solitude. The split-screen rhythm — outside vs inside — compressed to eight words. Forensic recognition of the CEO's actual day; dignity fully intact.
D"Everyone in the room needs your answer. No one asks what it costs you."
Formula: The Unnamed Observer. Describes his private reality so precisely it reads as telepathy. Status-safe — the cost is framed as the price of being needed, a strength.
E"Help was built for people who have someone above them. You don't."
Formula: The Exception Frame. Flatters via exclusion — ordinary support structurally cannot reach him — and positions the service as the thing built for the top chair. Pre-frames "why you and no one else."
F"The mind that built everything is carrying everything."
Formula: Strength-Turned-Cost. The Goldsmith inversion — the flaw IS the strength, so agreeing costs no status. Points at load and at the mind, the product's exact terrain.
G"Twelve clients. Nothing leaves the room."
Formula: Costly-Signal Understatement. The Rolls-Royce move — two flat, verifiable facts; premium implied by restraint, never declared. Capacity and confidentiality become the hook itself. Coldest of the eight; pairs best with a warm Recognition section.
H"The moment you arrived was the moment it got heavier."
Formula: The Arrival Paradox. The summit inverted — success delivered weight, not relief. Speaks to success-without-fulfillment and to inheritors as well as builders.
!Ruling needed: the voice register of the hookDecision
Standing decision: "We" always, never mixed with "you." But every proven hook in this space speaks "you" — recognition works in the second person; it is how the mirror works. Options: A) the hook (and only the hook) runs in "you", the body runs in We — the hook is the mirror, the site is the voice; B) full We-register everywhere, hooks recast (e.g. "We optimized everything except the minds running it"); C) subject-less hooks that dodge the choice entirely (candidates C and G already qualify). My recommendation: A — with the register border drawn cleanly at the hook.
The Ceremony

The Entrance Effect — First Load Only

Your directive, engineered to survive the evidence (E10). The law: the page paints fully underneath; the effect plays on a removable layer above it; any tap or scroll skips it; it runs once. Technically: a browser flag ("localStorage" — a small permanent note the browser keeps per site) is checked before first paint; first visit gets the ceremony, every visit after opens calm. A discreet replay control sits in the footer. Visitors whose devices request reduced motion get a simple fade. GSAP (the standard animation library) loads from CDN — no build tools, per standing decision.
AChampagne-dust convergenceCore recommendation
Black screen. Champagne particles drift like dust in light, then converge to trace the wordmark; a light sweep crosses the metal; the particles disperse and the veil dissolves into the already-painted hero. Canvas-based (a drawing surface the browser renders directly), ~400–700 particles — holds 60fps on mid-range phones. On-brand to the letter: gold dust on black.
BThe full ceremony — dust → headline build → light sweep → curtains partFireworks grade
Effect A extended into a 4–5 second sequence: particles converge, the Cormorant headline assembles letter by letter from blur, a champagne gradient sweeps the metal type, then two near-black silk panels part from a seam of light to reveal the live page. Phases run sequentially, never simultaneously — 60fps holds on mobile. The unveiling semantics fit a confidential practice precisely. This is the one that answers your "spectacular" demand in full.
CInk dissolve
A black veil dissolves like ink in water, champagne-tinted at the edges, revealing the hero. The biggest single cinematic image (WebGL — the browser's 3D/shader engine), but the largest engineering and testing surface, and it needs a fallback for older devices. Highest wow, highest cost.
→Recommendation
B — the full ceremony, with A as its core phase and a plain 400ms fade as the reduced-motion fallback. Real award-site precedents for every phase exist (Igloo Inc, Zentry, the Codrops curtain canon — see digest).
The Ask

The Form — Minimum Friction, Engineered

Your one-click law, checked against the evidence. Fewer fields converts more (3-field forms peak in a 40,000-page dataset; cutting 11→4 fields lifted conversions +120%) — but blind cutting is cargo-cult: a thoughtful qualifying question builds trust at this price point and filters for a twelve-client practice (P4). MECLABS resolved this exact tension: moving the heavy field to a second step lifted conversions +68%.
OptionMechanicsTrade
AOne fieldEmail only + button. Reply arrives personally.Absolute minimum friction; zero qualification, and an empty form can read as generic
BThree fieldsName · email · "What are you carrying right now?" (one open line)The dataset optimum; the question performs the service before it begins — but all friction is upfront
CTwo stepsStep 1: email + button — the commitment costs one field. Step 2 (after submit, optional): "If you want, say what prompted this." Minimum friction AND qualification — the +68% pattern. Slightly more build
→Recommendation
C. The ask itself stays one-click-one-field, honoring your friction law; the qualifying question arrives only after commitment, where it reads as care instead of cost. Around the form, always in the same breath: confidential · 20–30 minutes · here is what happens next (E8). No calendar widget on the page — inquiry first, scheduling in the reply (anti-patterns).
