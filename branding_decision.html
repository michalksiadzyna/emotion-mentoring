Verdict

1. The Recommendation

Option A, executed as a hybrid. Confidence: moderate-high (~75%).

Lead with the honest, owned category — Emotion Mentoring for CEOs — as the permanent masthead and land grab. And obey the one hard warning the entire case set gives: no winner ever leads with the raw emotional word alone. Every success welds it to performance or status in the same breath. So the first screen pairs the brand with a fused performance–emotion claim, and the credibility block (portrait, book, proof) sits immediately beneath — load-bearing, not decorative.

Why not B: "mind optimization" is not a known category — it gives the buyer no scaffolding, borrows biohacking connotations, and owns nothing. Pure benefit-led is the BetterUp play, and BetterUp's conditions (committee buyers, cold acquisition, stigma-safe procurement language) are the exact opposite of yours. You'd pay the full price of an invented category while gaining none of the differentiation.

The residual 25%: the one condition you only partially hold is pre-earned public authority (Colonna had a VC track record; Brown had 20 years of research data). The word carries best when the proof layer is visible on the same first screen — which our design already does. The book and the brand close this gap over time; it is the argument for the strategy, not against it.

Evidence

3. Taboo-Owning Brands That Won — and the Conditions

Jerry Colonna / Reboot — built the definitive "emotions for VCs" premium practice ("radical self-inquiry," trauma, Jungian depth). Instructive detail: his H1 today is the safe category ("CEO Coaching Services"); the soul lives in the subline ("Better Humans Make Better Leaders") and the material. The taboo is carried by the person and content, anchored by a scaffold the buyer already understands.

Morra Aarons-Mele, "The Anxious Achiever" — led with the stigmatized word and won (HBR Press, Thinkers50 2024). The mechanic: the taboo word is never naked — it is welded to a status word. Her headline: "High Performance Leadership Is Mentally Healthy Leadership."

Brené Brown / Dare to Lead — sold "vulnerability" into Shell and the Gates Foundation. Research authority de-risked the word; the cover still says "Dare to Lead" — courage outside, vulnerability inside.

Death doulas; menopause-in-corporate — genuinely avoided words went mainstream inside a decade, and the brands that said the word early now own the shelf (death doula membership 260 → 1,600+ in five years; menopause benefits 4% → 15% of employers in one year). First-movers in naming keep the vocabulary.

The counter-case, BetterUp — refused emotional framing, invented "mental fitness," won the enterprise market whose buyers are HR committees — and became interchangeable with the performance-platform crowd. Benefit-led wins committees; it forfeits distinctiveness.

The Law

4. The Winning Grammar

Across every success: [performance/status word] + [emotional word] welded into a single claim. "Anxious Achiever." "High Performance Leadership Is Mentally Healthy Leadership." "Better Humans Make Better Leaders." "Dare to Lead."

For us: "Emotion Mentoring for CEOs" already obeys the law — the taboo word and the status word in one breath. The first-screen claim beneath it fuses the adaptation promise with the emotional mechanism in one sentence (final copy drafted in the preview builds, We-voice, against the Canon).

Timing

5. The Stigma Is Falling — This Is Early, Not Late

55% of CEOs reported a mental-health issue in the past year — up 24 points in a single year; millennial CEOs highest at 60% (Businessolver 2024). Fortune documents male executives "rejecting the finance bro stereotype" at emotional-mastery retreats. 83% of Gen Z/millennials in therapy speak about it openly. Salesforce executives discuss therapy publicly and utilization runs 3× industry average.

The gap that makes this a land grab: private experience (55%) runs far ahead of public vocabulary — the buyer already knows the problem privately, and almost no premium brand names it. Trajectory is downward on stigma on every measured axis, steepest in the next CEO cohort. Owning the word now means owning the shelf when the vocabulary catches up — exactly the death-doula/menopause pattern.
